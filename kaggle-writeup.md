# BACKED

**A live market on whether you'll actually do the work.**

*Track: Motivation & Habits*

---

## The problem

Procrastination runs on shame, and shame is private. You avoid the task, feel bad about avoiding it, and the task becomes more aversive because it now carries the failure attached to it — so you avoid it harder. The loop tightens in secret, which is exactly why it survives. Commitment devices are the most evidenced intervention we have, but self-set stakes don't bind: you forgive yourself, quietly, every time. Study tools that answer questions don't touch any of this. They are, as the brief says, easy to build and easy to ignore.

## The emotional inversion

BACKED breaks the shame loop by pricing the avoidance before it happens. You commit to a study block; a prediction market opens on whether you'll finish it; your friends buy Yes or No; and you watch your own number. When the market has you at 31%, the thing you were hiding is already public, already quantified, and already priced — by people who like you and are not being cruel, just accurate. There is nothing left to conceal, so there is nothing left to be ashamed of. The private failure becomes a public number you can move.

That inversion — shame converted into stakes — is the emotional design of the whole product, and the copy enforces it. The interface never says *your friends think you'll fail*. It says *the market has you at 31%*. Market language is impersonal, and impersonal is what makes the number survivable. There are no exclamation marks and no encouragement anywhere in the app. The interface reports prices; it does not have feelings about you.

## The mechanic

The market must be *other people* betting on you. Self-priced commitments are gameable and self-forgiven; a market made by friends who know your record is honest by construction, and their positions give your commitment a cost of failure that you did not set and cannot quietly waive. The subject always holds a mandatory Yes position on their own market — you are structurally long on yourself — and settlement moves a persistent leaderboard, so every market updates your reputation, which updates your future odds.

## Gemma 4 as book, coach and oracle

**Architecture.** A dependency-free static frontend (vanilla JS, opens from `file://`) talks to a ~100-line Python stdlib engine on localhost, which calls **`gemma-4-26b-a4b-it`** via the Gemini API. We chose the 26B MoE deliberately: ~4B active parameters gives near-instant generation for the on-stage calls while keeping 26B-class reasoning for pricing. Every model response is cached by input hash, and every call has a hardcoded fallback — the demo cannot hard-fail.

Gemma has four jobs, with thinking mode split deliberately across them:

**1. The book** (`thinking: high`). Before every market, Gemma reads a per-learner JSON model — completion rates by topic, by hour, by block size, last seven results, a drift profile, and an attempt log — and returns a calibrated probability with one line of bookmaker rationale. Fed a novel commitment ("Linear algebra coursework 2 — eigenvalue proofs", 45 min at 11:00) it priced 40%: *"You're 2-from-7 in your last seven and your completion rate in the 30–60m bracket is only 48%."*

**2. Adaptive pacing — the advisory counter-offer** (`thinking: high`). If the record shows the same topic, hour and size failed three or more times, Gemma refuses to recommend the market and counters with a winnable one, citing the record: *"you're 0-for-4 in thermodynamics at 16:00 over 60m; 7-from-9 in the 10:00 under-30m slot."* Crucially, the counter is advisory: the learner defines their market and can open it anyway — at the punitive line Gemma quotes for insisting (5%, for that record). The app never overrides the user; the market simply makes ignoring your own history expensive.

**3. The margin call** (`thinking: off`, for speed). Mid-session, idle time drifts the price down publicly. At a threshold, a deterministic timer triggers one generated nudge in a trading-desk voice — the idle minutes, the live price, and the single smallest action that recovers the position: *"Nine minutes idle, price is 24, and Sara is adding to her No. One trade gets you back over thirty."* It arrives at the exact moment of avoidance, it uses the market itself as the pressure source, and it never scolds.

**4. The oracle** (`thinking: high`, multimodal). At resolution the learner photographs their worked pages and Gemma judges whether genuine engagement with the topic occurred at roughly the committed effort — explicitly *not* grading correctness, because a page of wrong working is still work. It resolves YES/NO with confidence and a one-line public verdict. Settlement arithmetic then runs in plain code — payouts are never generated by the model.

Remove Gemma and there is no product: the odds, the pacing decision, the nudge and the verdict all disappear.

## The oracle problem

Resolution is the hardest component of any prediction market, and ours resolves on "did this person actually study" — a claim with no ground truth anywhere. It is gameable: you can photograph someone else's notes. We say so plainly, and respond with design rather than denial. First, adversarial resolution: any No-holder can challenge a verdict, and Gemma then generates a targeted comprehension question on the material — the people financially motivated to catch fraud do the catching, and the model adjudicates (designed and prompted in this sprint; not wired into the demo). Second, reputation compounds: thin evidence tanks your form, which widens your future lines and shrinks every future payout — the market prices dishonesty automatically. The oracle also discriminates in practice: fed a blank image, it returned *NO, confidence 100: "the provided image is a blank solid color and contains no evidence of work performed."* And there is a quiet pedagogical bonus: proving you studied requires articulating what you learned, so the verification step is itself retrieval practice.

## Meeting the brief

The app answers no study questions at all. It fights procrastination with the most evidenced device there is — a commitment contract with social stakes — and keeps learners on track by making off-track time visible and expensive in real time. It nudges at the right moment: the margin call fires at the moment of avoidance, not on a schedule. It adapts to how you're progressing on two loops — within-session (drift and nudge respond to live activity) and between-session (today's settlement is tomorrow's odds). And it holds learners accountable to their own goals, literally: your own stated commitment, priced by people who know you, settled in public.

## Sprint scope and challenges

Three Gemma calls are genuinely live in the demo — pricing/counter-offer, the margin call, and multimodal resolution. Counterparties are simulated in this sprint: positions, price impact, drift and settlement are deterministic code. Challenges overcome in the day: Gemma 4 returns internal `thought` parts that must be filtered from responses; JSON arrives fence-wrapped and is parsed defensively with per-call fallbacks; free-tier rate limits forced input-hash caching with exponential backoff; and our single-threaded demo server deadlocked a slow multimodal call behind a nudge until we made it threaded.

## Track justification

Streaks: the seven-square form line has market consequences — runs tighten your line and raise payouts. Accountability mechanics: the market itself. Adaptive pacing: the counter-offer, personalised from history and priced rather than preached. The emotional side of procrastination: the shame-to-stakes inversion above, enforced by every line of interface copy.

## Risks and responsible design

No randomness, no chasing, no re-staking, non-monetary stakes — and the house warns you off bets your record says you'll lose, which no real bookmaker would ever do. It is a commitment contract with price discovery, built to make follow-through, not wagering, the habit.
